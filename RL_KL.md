We simplify the question, we have questions represented by $x$ and answers $y$. Given that we have the data distributions $p_{\rm data}(x, y)$ and $p_{\rm data}(y | x)$, the target of learning/training is utilizing the model parametrized by $\theta$ to model $p_{\rm data}(y | x)$, i.e., $p_{\theta}(y | x)\Rightarrow p_{\rm data}(y | x)$.

With KL divergence (you can also use Wasserstein/TV distances if you can compute these efficiently), we have the following optimization problem
$$
	\min_{\theta} \mathcal{L}(y,x;\theta):=KL(p_{\rm data}(y | x) \| p_{\theta}(y | x))
$$
$$
	\min_{\theta} \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} p_{\rm data}(y|x)\log{\frac{p_{\rm data}(y | x)}{p_{\theta}(y | x)}} \mathrm{d}y \\ \min_{\theta} \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \underbrace{p_{\rm data}(y|x)\log{p_{\rm data}(y | x)}}_{\rm Constant\ term} - p_{\rm data}(y|x)\log{p_{\theta}(y | x)}\mathrm{d}y \\ \min_{\theta} \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} -p_{\rm data}(y | x)\log{ p_{\theta}(y | x) } \mathrm{d}y \Rightarrow \max_{\theta} \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} p_{\rm data}(y | x)\log{ p_{\theta}(y | x) } \mathrm{d}y
$$
Thus, $\min_{\theta} \mathcal{L}(y,x; \theta)$ is equivalent to $\max_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim p_{\rm data}(y | x)}[\log{p_{\theta}(y | x) }]$. Let us get the first-order gradient of the problem:
$$
\nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{L}(y,x ; \theta)}=\mathbb{E}_{y \sim p_{\rm data}(y | x)}[\log{p_{\theta}(y | x) }]= \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} p_{\rm data}(y | x) \nabla_{\theta}{\log{ p_{\theta}(y | x) }} \mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \underbrace{\frac{ p_{ \rm data }(y | x) }{ p_{ \theta }(y | x) }}_{\rm Importance\ sampling\ ration} \nabla_{\theta}{p_{\theta}(y | x) }\mathrm{d}y
$$
Now we have the first-order gradient under the perspective of supervised learning, where you have correct answers $p_{\rm data}( y | x )$. 

Recall the policy gradient of RL
$$
\nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{J}(y, x; \theta)} := \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_{\theta}(\tau)}[\sum_{t=0}^{T}{A(s_t, a_t)}\nabla_{\theta}{\log{\pi_{\theta}(a_t | s_t)}} ] = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_{\theta}(\tau)}[ \sum_{t=0}^{T}{A(y_t, x_t)}\nabla_{\theta}{\log{\pi_{\theta}(y_t | x_t)}} ]
$$
The trajectories $\tau$ can be expanded as $\tau=(x_0, y_0, x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2,...,x_T,y_T)$, generated by the model $\pi_{\theta}$.  $\nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{J}(y,x; \theta)}$ can further be written as
$$
\nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{J}(y, x; \theta)} = \int_{\tau} \sum_{t=0}^{T} \Big[{A(y_t, x_t)}\nabla_{\theta}{\log{\pi_{\theta}(y_t | x_t)}}\Big] \mathrm{d}\tau  \\  \nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{J}(y, x; \theta)} = \sum_{t=0}^{T}{\int_{y_t, x_t} \pi_{\theta}(y_t, x_t) A(y_t, x_t) \nabla_{\theta}(\log{\pi_{\theta}(y_t | x_t)})  \mathrm{d}y_t\mathrm{d}x_t}  \\  \nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{J}(y, x; \theta)} = \sum_{t=0}^{T}{\int_{y_t , x_t} \pi_{\theta}(y_t | x_t) \underbrace{\pi_{\theta}(x_t)}_{\rm State\ Transition} A(y_t, x_t) \nabla_{\theta}(\log{\pi_{\theta}(y_t | x_t)})  \mathrm{d}y_t\mathrm{d}x_t}   \\  \nabla_{\theta}{\mathcal{J}(y, x; \theta)} = \sum_{t=0}^{T}{\int_{y_t , x_t}  \underbrace{ \pi_{\theta}(x_t)  A(y_t, x_t)}_{_{\rm Weights\ of\ gradients}} \nabla_{\theta}(\pi_{\theta}(y_t | x_t) )  \mathrm{d}y_t\mathrm{d}x_t}
$$

Comparing the gradients of supervised learning and RL, we can obtain the key differences:

  1. The gradients of RL are with time-cumulative effects and something like **process-level** supervision. The gradients of supervised learning only use labels $p_{\rm data}(y | x)$ and are **outcome-level**.
  2. The gradients of RL can be interpreted as how to approximate the **weights** of $\nabla_{\theta}{\pi_{\theta}}$, where we cannot access to labels like $p_{\rm data}(y | x)$. Whereas supervised learning only utilize the ratio $\frac{p_{\rm data} }{ p_{\theta}(y | x) }$ as the **weight** of $\nabla_{\theta}{p_{\theta}}$.
  3. The weight of gradients in RL considers the information about the **future** (i.e., from $t$ to $T$, recall the definition of the Advantage term $A(y_t, x_t)$) of the trajectories. Supervised learning only dives into the current step $t$. Some optimization algorithms considers future steps, e.g., Nesterov, shows better global properties. 
  4. Trajectories of RL are generated by the model $\theta$ (KL), whereas trajectories/samples of supervised learning are sampled from $p_{\rm data}(y | x)$ (reverse KL). That is an important point related KL/reverse KL. In generative models, samples sampled from the real/data distribution (paradigm like supervised learning) can achieve better performance, e.g., Sinkhorn solving or Iterative Proportional Fitting. About reverse KL, please refer to https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/on-policy-distillation.

Overall, if a bridge between two types of weights of supervised learning and RL can be built, maybe we can derive algorithms from the perspective of "weights of gradients".
